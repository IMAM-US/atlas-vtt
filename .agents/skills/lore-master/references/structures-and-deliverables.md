# Structures and deliverables

Choose a structure because it supports the table's intended decisions. The formats here are adaptable working aids, not mandatory schemas. They are Lore Master's synthesis rather than copied templates from a book.

## Match form to play

| Form | What players decide | Useful preparation | Common fragility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Site/dungeon | Where to go and how to engage its contents | Entrances, routes, keyed spaces, occupants, hazards, discoveries, retreat | A corridor of mandatory fights with no consequential route choice |
| Investigation | What evidence means and which lead to pursue | Truth, timeline, suspects, revelation table, independent clues | One indispensable witness, roll, or interpretation |
| Heist/raid | How to gain access, reach an objective, and leave | Security layers, schedules, vulnerabilities, resources, alarms, escape routes | The GM's single expected plan is the only viable method |
| Journey/exploration | Which route, pace, preparation, and destination | Landmarks, route tradeoffs, travel rules, local encounters, discoveries | Repetitive rolls without decisions or changed circumstances |
| Intrigue | Whom to trust, support, bargain with, or expose | Faction goals, dependencies, information flows, leverage, public/private positions | Everyone knows everything and betrays the party automatically |
| Episodic mission | How to accomplish a clear local objective | Entry, bounded situation, obstacles, possible outcomes, aftermath | Each episode returns to the same state regardless of choices |
| Sandbox | Which opportunities deserve attention | Actionable hooks, meaningful destinations, faction motion, consequences | Unlimited geography with no legible opportunities |
| Campaign arc | What commitments and transformations emerge over time | Central questions, recurring people/places, escalating pressures, local closures | A finale that requires fixed PC decisions across many sessions |

Use a hybrid when its parts interact: an investigation reveals heist access; a journey changes political leverage; dungeon discoveries alter a faction dispute. Avoid requiring every structure in one adventure.

## Minimal useful material by request

- **Lore entry:** requested history or identity, present traces, competing interpretations when useful, connections to existing canon, and possibilities for discovery. No mandatory full adventure.
- **Location:** immediate description, usable features and routes, occupants, secrets, pressures, and relevant connections.
- **NPC/faction:** motives, leverage, limits, relationships, knowledge, and reaction tendencies.
- **Obstacle:** goal, constraints, signals, approaches, stakes, consequences, and recovery/bypass where relevant.
- **Mystery:** underlying truth, actual evidence, independent access paths, leads, and effects of discovery.
- **Session packet:** current situation, quick entry, likely useful places/people, clues, challenges, current pressure, rewards, and conditional aftermath.
- **Campaign codex:** organizing truths and history, geography, societies and powers, recurring cast, supernatural rules, central tensions/questions, adventure opportunities, continuity practices, and the next playable situation.

Match breadth to the requested result. A campaign codex can be rich and still begin with a compact index and summary. A one-shot should reach playable material quickly.

## A useful adventure packet

When the user asks for a complete adventure and gives no format, begin with a DM overview:

1. Premise and intended experience, scope/duration assumptions, system/party tuning if known.
2. What is happening now, underlying cause, and why the PCs might engage.
3. Main people/powers, useful locations and connections, and active pressure.
4. Important questions and possible outcomes, including failure or withdrawal.

Follow with only the cards and evidence needed to run it. Use stable short labels or IDs when a network is complex enough to need cross-references. A simple village encounter does not need a database.

### Location/scene card

Name and access; public observable description; interactable features; occupants and behavior; GM secrets and evidence; risks; exits/connections; changes after intervention. Include actual details rather than instructions to invent interesting scenery later.

### NPC card

Role and cue; immediate desire; knowledge/misbeliefs; leverage and limits; terms/concessions; likely responses. Important dialogue can be a short cue or quotation written for this NPC, with no imposed PC reply.

### Obstacle card

Objective; fictional cause; signals; approaches with different costs; resolution through the chosen system; concrete success/setback; changed situation. Indicate when mechanical values remain untuned.

### Evidence card or revelation table

Discovery location/method; actual observable content; truth/claim status; intended implication; lead or use; who knows it. Separate essential evidence from optional depth. Make independent access paths explicit.

### Pressure/aftermath card

Tracked state; advancement triggers; warning signs; what completion changes; intervention effects. Then conditional outcomes with rewards, obligations, affected relationships, and next opportunities. Do not write future outcomes as completed events.

## Player and GM editions

Identify the intended audience before preparing excerpts. Player material can contain observations, known history, offers, rumors, and claims appropriate to what the PCs know. Rumors are presented as rumors rather than accompanied by GM truth labels.

GM material can contain hidden causes, answers, unrevealed identities, motives, clue implications, and conditional plans. A player packet must not reveal these through titles, links, footnotes, comments, summaries, or metadata. An apparent public heading can itself be a spoiler.

Player recaps include actual events and information known to the players. Do not mention an abandoned encounter, discarded ending, or unrevealed preparation to explain why the next session changed. Check each recap statement against what actually happened or was revealed.

Drafting a player excerpt is not permission to distribute it. Preserve the requested publication boundary. If the user asks only for DM preparation, no player-facing export is necessary.

## Revision and continuity

Identify the changed element and its dependents before rewriting. A relocated ruin may affect routes and travel; a changed culprit may affect physical evidence and witnesses; a freed spirit may invalidate future binding scenes. Repair those dependencies while preserving unaffected canon.

Lead a revision with what changed and its practical effects. List genuinely unresolved contradictions. Keep in-world claims separate from author errors. A narrow change should not rename the world, invent PC history, or rewrite unrelated factions.

When continuing an established campaign, maintain a small accepted-facts/change record if the user wants saved artifacts: additions adopted, events actually resolved, secrets revealed, relationships/resources changed, pending decisions, and abandoned future preparation. Use existing campaign records rather than creating a competing canonical writer.

## Critique and learning

Evaluate concrete table behavior: what becomes inaccessible, which choice lacks information, which motive fails, or which aftermath ignores player action. Show the smallest repair and explain its effect. Avoid taste judgments dressed as universal requirements.

After a session, use actual feedback and events to refine the next packet: what players pursued, where they stalled, what they cared about, and what changed. A simulated walk-through can find dependencies; it cannot establish that a real group will enjoy the adventure.
