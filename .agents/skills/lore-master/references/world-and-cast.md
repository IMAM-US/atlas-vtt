# World and cast

This is original craft synthesis. Kevin Crawford's *Worlds Without Number* supports broad worldbuilding tools; Sly Flourish's secrets approach supports discoverable lore. Use their specific techniques through the source links in the entrypoint's resource library, without claiming this checklist is theirs.

## Build a world that produces situations

Choose a few organizing truths that explain many details. Connect them through causes:

`terrain and resources -> livelihoods and routes -> institutions and dependencies -> contested interests -> present opportunities`

History enters that chain where it changes what people inherit: a border, an oath, a ruin, a debt, a technology, a taboo, or a scar. Avoid a chronology of rulers that never affects the present. When the user wants deep history for its own sake, provide it, but retain clear relationships between eras.

For a place, establish:

- What sustains it: food, water, labor, trade, protection, or magic.
- Who controls access and who maintains the systems others depend on.
- What makes ordinary life distinctive: one custom, sensory motif, recurring task, or social expectation.
- What is changing now, who benefits, and who pays.
- What outsiders can observe and what they might misunderstand.

Use concrete details with consequences. A city built around seasonal flood pillars suggests raised homes, ferry guilds, water-height records, evacuation routes, and political disputes about maintenance. Its distinctiveness does work across exploration, conflict, and lore.

## Geography and scale

Keep parent/child places, travel links, routes, and barriers consistent. Describe relative placement and approximate travel demands when exact maps are absent. Do not invent measured geometry or a hidden passage that contradicts a confirmed map.

Develop at the scale of the decision:

| Scale | Useful design questions |
| --- | --- |
| World/region | What separates peoples, sustains trade, or limits travel? What is changing across borders? |
| Settlement | Which districts and institutions matter? Who can provide help, access, or opposition? |
| Site | What entrances, routes, inhabitants, resources, and hazards affect an approach? |
| Immediate scene | What can be perceived, reached, manipulated, negotiated, or lost now? |

One scale need not be completed before another. A rich local place can imply a larger world through imported goods, migrant stories, religious practice, and old construction.

## History, myth, and discovery

For important history, record the event, its surviving trace, current consequence, competing interpretation, and who knows what. Build at least one way players can encounter it without a lecture. A trace can be a changed riverbed, reused stones, a song, a scar, an obligation, or an artifact.

Distinguish true history from official history and folk explanation. Label discrepancies in GM material. An unreliable account needs a reason and discoverable evidence; casual contradiction does not create a good mystery.

Use layered depth: what everyone recognizes; what a specialist can explain; what evidence complicates; what remains unknown. Give useful information before obscure names. A revelation should clarify stakes or open possibilities, while some unanswered questions can preserve wonder.

When drawing from real cultures or history, specify which features inspire the invention. Preserve internal diversity and avoid treating a living culture as a bundle of exotic costumes or a single moral alignment. Verify factual claims when they matter; invented adaptations must be labeled.

## Cosmology, religion, and magic

Define only what the requested material needs: what powers exist, what they can affect, how people understand them, what access costs, and what constrains them. Establish whether divine facts are known, debated, or deliberately mysterious.

Check a central world rule against the opening's observations and residents' ordinary use of it. Explain exceptions when they affect the mystery or obstacle. An unexplained change in how a power works can make the apparent evidence contradict the intended cause.

Consider consequences beyond combat. Reliable healing changes institutions; resurrection changes succession; teleportation changes borders; divination changes investigations. If the campaign has these capabilities, design conflicts they do not trivially negate or let their use meaningfully solve the problem. Do not add a convenient immunity solely to protect a plot.

Religions can contain rites, places, ethics, social services, dissent, and political interests. Their members need not share one interpretation. A supernatural mystery can leave ultimate origins unknown while keeping its immediate behavior consistent.

## Factions as sources of motion

Give an active faction:

| Field | Useful content |
| --- | --- |
| Desire | A concrete change it seeks or condition it defends |
| Reason | Why that outcome matters to its members |
| Leverage | People, money, authority, information, infrastructure, force, or magic it can use |
| Limits | What it cannot know, afford, control, or tolerate |
| Dependency | Who or what it relies on |
| Division | An internal disagreement that matters, if one is useful |
| Current move | What it is doing now and what enables its next step |
| Response | How it might react to help, interference, bargaining, or exposure |

Choose relationships that generate play: alliance with a price, disputed resource, fragile truce, mutual dependence, infiltration, or competing duties. Factions need not all be villains. Neither complexity nor moral ambiguity is mandatory for every game.

Information travels through people and channels. A faction cannot react to a secret player act until it has a plausible way to learn of it. A plan also requires time, access, personnel, and resources; it can fail independently of the PCs.

## NPCs players can work with

For an important NPC, supply name/role, immediate desire, broader stake, usable leverage, knowledge and mistaken beliefs, red line, likely concession, and one performable cue. Describe what they do when pressure changes. Avoid a page of biography before the DM knows how to play them.

A cue can be a habit, verbal rhythm, preferred image, or practical concern. It need not be a theatrical accent. Attach secrets and clues to knowledge the NPC plausibly possesses.

Separate a character's want from their need. A gatekeeper might demand an official seal while actually needing assurance that a rescue party will return. This creates room for bargaining. It does not imply that every obstacle can be talked away.

Recurring characters accumulate history with the PCs. Let favors, betrayals, victories, and losses change their behavior. A defeated minor opponent may become important; a planned major villain may die early. Prepare consequences instead of guaranteed escapes.

## Creatures and encounters

Give creatures a place in the world: habitat, food or energy, behavior, signs of presence, territory, relationships, and reasons to approach or avoid people. Supernatural beings may follow symbolic or magical laws rather than biological ecology, but those laws should remain intelligible.

Behavior produces choices. A guardian defending eggs, a hungry scavenger, a bound sentinel, and a pursuing hunter make different encounters even with similar statistics. For mechanical construction, use the campaign's system and capabilities rather than inventing generic challenge ratings.

## Objects, treasure, and rewards

An important object can connect maker, past use, present owner, recognizable features, practical function, and competing claims. Avoid giving every ordinary object a secret dynasty.

Vary rewards: equipment, wealth, information, safe routes, allies, shelter, status, institutional access, restored places, and player-owned accomplishments. Ensure a reward is usable or clearly promises a later opportunity. Specify custody and obligations when they matter.

Use system-appropriate item mechanics only when requested and supported. Distinguish a fictional property from a verified rule effect. A quest item should not silently remove ordinary character capabilities.

## Character ties and emotional stakes

Start from player-established interests, relationships, goals, and past decisions. Offer optional connections when none are supplied. Do not invent a PC's secret parentage, crime, vow, trauma, or destiny as accepted canon.

Make attachments through interaction: a helpful person with a distinct need, a place the PCs improved, a promise they chose, or a rival they understand. Threatening those attachments can create stakes, but repeated hostage-taking quickly becomes predictable and punitive.

Leave room for joy, celebration, humor, craft, beauty, and belonging. A world players wish to preserve needs experiences they enjoy having in it.
