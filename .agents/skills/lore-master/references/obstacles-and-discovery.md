# Obstacles and discovery

This guide combines original design practice with Justin Alexander's Three Clue Rule and node-based scenario design for robust discovery, and nonlinear dungeon design for meaningful routes. Apply their reasoning where a bottleneck actually exists.

## Design the problem, its signals, and its consequences

An obstacle card should let the DM answer:

| Question | Required when relevant |
| --- | --- |
| What is blocked? | A goal, resource, route, understanding, or relationship |
| Why? | Physical conditions, competing interests, uncertainty, or a rule of the world |
| What can players perceive? | Signs, leverage, limits, danger, and useful information |
| What approaches make sense? | A few examples with different costs; remain open to others |
| What changes on success? | Concrete access, knowledge, position, safety, or influence |
| What changes on setback? | A consequence consistent with the approach and stakes |
| Can players leave or try elsewhere? | Retreat, bypass, recovery, or a different objective |

Different methods should matter. Bribery may preserve stealth but create a debt; force may save time but attract pursuit. Three cosmetic routes with the same forced outcome provide little agency. Do not require every small obstacle to have three solutions; reserve redundancy for essential gates.

Stakes should be observable or discoverable before commitment where the fiction allows. Surprise can reveal a hidden danger, but usable clues and consistent behavior make it fair. Once a consequence is established, do not change it to punish success or rescue prepared content.

## Social obstacles

Prepare positions and underlying interests, information possessed, concessions, authority, constraints, and ways to demonstrate credibility. Identify who can actually grant the desired outcome. Let a negotiation change terms, access, alliances, or risk.

Deception and coercion require plausible leverage and consequences. Do not decide a PC's voluntary promise, belief, or speech. Apply the chosen game's social and magical rules rather than treating every conversation as a universal persuasion roll. A faction's red line can remain firm even when a player rolls well.

## Combat with a purpose

Establish what each side is trying to accomplish beyond eliminating hit points: escape, protect someone, delay pursuit, seize a device, interrupt a ritual, capture a witness, or hold ground. Objectives can be straightforward; heroic battle need not become moral negotiation.

Use terrain and interaction when they matter: cover, elevation, hazards, moving machinery, fragile structures, civilians, limited visibility, or changing access. Supply signals and usable responses. Do not flood the encounter with unrelated gimmicks.

Prepare opponent behavior, communications, morale, retreat conditions, and reinforcements with plausible triggers. Consider party abilities and action economy before claiming difficulty. Unknown system/level means the tactical situation is authored but numerical balance remains unverified.

Accept bypass, surrender, alliance, or early victory when they follow the situation. Never silently increase durability or invent an escape to preserve a boss scene.

## Exploration and travel

Make route decisions use information. Routes can differ by distance, exposure, supplies, access, navigation, weather, encounters, and knowledge of the area. Provide landmarks and ways to learn about those differences. A travel montage is suitable when no interesting decision remains.

For sites, use entrances, loops, cross-connections, vantage points, shortcuts, and distinct regions where exploration is the intended experience. Keep connections spatially credible. An alternate path should change what can be avoided, discovered, or approached differently.

Encounters should show local life or emerging conditions as well as provide danger. Select or roll from authored tables according to the agreed method; do not invent a die result. Resource pressure must follow the campaign's travel rules and scale.

## Mysteries and clue networks

Start with the underlying truth and causal sequence. Establish who did what, why, how, when, what traces remain, and what witnesses can know. Then list the conclusions needed for progress, distinguishing them from optional depth.

For each essential conclusion, use a revelation table:

| Conclusion | Evidence | Where/how discoverable | What it establishes | Further lead |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A clear statement | A specific observation, record, testimony, or reaction | A plausible independent access path | Distinguish proof from suggestion | A person, place, test, or choice |

Following the Three Clue Rule, provide at least three clues for an essential conclusion. Three sentences in the same locked diary share one access failure; diversify sources and methods. Check the scenario as a network of locations/people/events and leads. A dead end may offer flavor or reward, but the whole investigation should not hinge on finding it.

If accepted canon makes a particular record or item unique, preserve that constraint. Provide independent access routes, permitted corroboration, or ways to investigate its contents instead of inventing duplicate identifying evidence. Source redundancy is a design aid; it does not authorize rewriting fixed facts.

Essential evidence should be obtainable through appropriate investigation; use rolls for uncertainty, speed, detail, exposure, or other system-supported consequences where suitable. Do not make the campaign's only way forward a single perception roll. When a lead is missed, use an independently plausible lead or consequences that create a new opportunity. Do not teleport an established document to wherever the PCs look.

Sly Flourish's flexible secrets are useful for information whose delivery method has not been fixed. Distinguish that pool from placed physical evidence, witness knowledge, and causal facts already established. Flexibility in presentation cannot rewrite confirmed evidence or guarantee one interpretation.

Red herrings are optional. If used, make them resolvable with available evidence and keep enough true leads accessible. A mistaken witness needs a reason. The GM should know which claims are false and how players could check them.

A revelation is valuable when it changes understanding or a decision. Include actual clue content, not merely an instruction to give the players a clue. Give the DM both surface observation and intended implication; players may infer something else.

## Puzzles

Specify the goal, visible components, stable operating rule, feedback, relevant clues, intended solution, and reasonable alternate solutions or bypasses. Work through the logic before delivery. A puzzle must not depend on an unstated fact only the author knows.

Distinguish character competence from player puzzle-solving. Allow the table's agreed hint, skill, or accessibility approaches. State whether a puzzle is optional or essential. For essential puzzles, provide a credible way to learn the rule or proceed by another method.

Accept an alternate solution if it follows the established rules. Do not change the answer after the players reason correctly. A riddle answer can remain specific; a plausible different answer earns clarification or usable feedback rather than arbitrary punishment.

## Traps and hazards

Specify signs, trigger, target/effect, timing, consequence, ways to investigate or disable, and what happens on partial progress. Physical and magical mechanisms should explain their behavior. Include how the trap was maintained or why an ancient mechanism still works when that affects credibility.

A hazard can ask whether to cross, how to protect someone, what to sacrifice, or whether to seek a safer route. Signal consequential danger through damage patterns, remnants, sound, inscriptions, witnesses, or active demonstrations. Hidden danger is part of some genres; calibrate its severity and detectability to the table's expectations.

## Failure, recovery, and ingenuity

Failures can spend time, consume equipment, worsen position, damage trust, attract attention, separate people, lose an opportunity, or change the objective. Match consequence to action. Not every failure needs to advance the plot, and not every success needs a complication.

Keep a recoverable path when the adventure promises continuation, but allow irreversible stakes the group accepted. When defeat ends this mission, offer the resulting world state rather than disguising failure as predetermined success.

For an unexpected solution, check the fiction, available resources, and rules. If it works, let it work. Prepare the reaction of affected people and systems instead of moving the same obstacle back in front of the party.
