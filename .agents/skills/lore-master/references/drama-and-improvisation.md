# Drama and improvisation

This is original synthesis informed by situation-based preparation, *Dungeon World* fronts, *Blades in the Dark* clocks, Fate's scenario questions, and Robin D. Laws' hope/fear pacing lens. These are optional techniques with different assumptions, not interchangeable rules.

## Plot as plans and resulting events

Distinguish three uses of plot:

- A person's scheme: something you can prepare, including its resources and weaknesses.
- An adventure outline: likely opportunities, locations, and pressures, with outcomes open.
- The sequence of events that actually occurred: something you record after choices and resolution.

Avoid turning the second into a mandated version of the third. A clear mission or shared campaign premise is compatible with freedom over approaches and outcomes. Respect a group's preferred degree of guidance.

For an active scheme, establish goal, current move, enabling resources, information limits, observable signs, and the state it would reach without intervention. Note reactions as tendencies rather than exhaustive branches. A defeated organizer can leave debts, exposed allies, abandoned projects, or a power vacuum, without an automatic replacement villain.

## Questions that sustain play

Write consequential questions you are willing to leave unanswered: who receives the disputed inheritance, whether the village survives, what price a faction will accept, or what the party chooses to protect. Fate's scenario approach is a useful source for building problems and opposition around such questions.

Give major questions intermediate discoveries and decisions. A campaign arc needs opportunities for progress before its finale. Each session should allow some local closure even when the larger question remains open.

Themes emerge through recurring tensions: duty and freedom, memory and truth, belonging and ambition. Put those tensions in people, places, and decisions. Do not dictate the PCs' moral conclusions or punish them for rejecting the author's preferred answer.

## Scenes as opportunities

Prepare a scene's current state, meaningful goal/question, people and pressures, observable detail, interactable features, and conditions that could change or end it. Identify access and exit where they matter. A location can host different scenes as its state changes.

Opening scenes should give the players something to act on quickly: an interrupted routine, a request with a price, a visible problem, an unusual discovery, or an opportunity. Immediate combat is only one option.

Use familiar dramatic structures as lenses. An opening, complication, discovery, confrontation, and aftermath can help identify missing opportunities; the players may reorder or bypass them. Do not require every adventure to follow acts, a hero's journey, or a predetermined character transformation.

## Pacing and emotional texture

Robin D. Laws' *Hamlet's Hit Points* examines changes in hope and fear across story beats. Borrow that lens to notice monotony: endless worsening pressure, effortless victories, or revelations with no time to react. The publisher overview supports this lens; reproducing its full taxonomy requires access to the book.

Vary tension with discovery, accomplishment, connection, humor, wonder, and recovery. Let important decisions and losses breathe. Pace for the table's response and remaining session time, rather than enforcing a beat count.

Use recurring motifs as reminders with changed meaning. A festival song can begin as local color and later reveal a neglected promise. A return to a saved settlement can show real consequences. Payoffs should make earlier details newly intelligible while preserving what those details established.

## Revelations and twists

A fair reveal has a coherent truth, earlier traces, a reason it was hidden or misunderstood, and consequences worth acting on. Ask whether the players could retrospectively recognize the evidence. Surprising connections are stronger than facts that contradict confirmed events.

Let a discovery happen early if players earn it. Do not relocate a culprit, change a locked answer, or erase an NPC's knowledge because the reveal was scheduled for later. The new information can shift the challenge from discovery to action.

If a reveal would impose a PC relationship, identity, belief, or past action, offer it as a proposal unless already authorized. A secret about an NPC does not grant authority to rewrite a player character.

## Pressure tools

**Fronts:** borrow the idea of linked threats, warning signs, and possible worsening outcomes from *Dungeon World*. Use when several dangers need continuity. Keep stakes questions open and revise future steps after actual intervention. Adapting fronts to another game does not import Dungeon World's resolution rules.

**Clocks:** borrow *Blades in the Dark* progress clocks when effort or trouble develops over time. Name the state being tracked, define what advances/reduces it, and state what completion means. Track the fictional changes; do not advance a clock merely because players are discussing an interesting choice. Use no clock when a simple condition or event is enough.

In either case, connect escalation to elapsed time, enabling events, action results, or other agreed triggers. Different pressures may interact, but a vast dashboard is unnecessary for one village problem. The DM needs to know what can change and why.

## Improvise from constraints

Graham Walmsley's *Play Unsafe* is a reading resource for improvisation. The following loop is Lore Master's own practical synthesis:

1. Recover established facts, current pressure, NPC motives/resources, and the player's declared intent.
2. Determine what follows plausibly from the proposed action. Preserve rules and visible stakes; ask only for outcome-changing missing information.
3. Reuse compatible details. Add only what the immediate response needs, distinguishing a new proposal from an established fact when working out of character.
4. Suggest or narrate the response within the requested role. Keep the player's next decision open and defer unresolved rolls to the live-play workflow.
5. Record adopted additions and resolved consequences in the campaign's appropriate artifact when persistence is requested.

Accept player contributions according to the table's authorship agreement. An inspired suggestion need not become hidden canon automatically. Offer a tradeoff, clarify a constraint, or admit a short preparation pause when improvising would break important facts.

## Adapt common disruptions

| Player action | Compatible design response |
| --- | --- |
| Ignore the hook | Preserve their chosen goal; allow unattended events to change only through credible causes; supply a different relevant opportunity if appropriate |
| Miss a clue | Use another authored source, an investigative lead, or a new event that plausibly creates access |
| Resolve the climax early | Honor the result; develop aftermath, remaining obligations, or closure; discard obsolete preparation |
| Befriend an opponent | Reassess that NPC's interests and limits; let the alliance alter actual access and opposition |
| Split the party | Offer distinct information and decisions, manage spotlight, and avoid forcing reunification through unexplained danger |
| Propose a new explanation | Check it against fixed evidence; adopt compatible open details if useful, while preserving truths already locked |

None of these responses requires preserving the original finale. Continuity and meaningful consequences are enough to produce the next part of the story.
